From Cyber Nation to AI Nation: Can Israel Reinvent Its Brand?

April 4, 2026 · Max Matkovski

For the past two decades, when the world thinks about Israeli tech, one word comes to mind above all others : cybersecurity.

From elite military units to venture capital backing and global corporate demand, Israel has become the undisputed leader in defending digital worlds. But as we enter an era defined by artificial intelligence, it is increasingly clear that AI is not cyber and that Israel’s cyber-centric identity may be a double-edged sword.

While cybersecurity has allowed Israel to become the world’s premier digital sentry, the AI era demands a shift from a defensive posture to a generative one, moving from protecting the world’s data to architecting its intelligence.

Why Cyber Remains on Top

Israeli cybersecurity dominates. Cyber companies made up only roughly 7% of the tech ecosystem in 2024 yet captured nearly 40% of all tech investment. Clearly, a small portion of startups continues to capture a disproportionate share of investment capital, focusing funding into a single narrow vertical. Deep technical talent from elite intelligence units, early global validation, and international demand created fertile ground for rapid growth.

By mid-2025, fundraising for cybersecurity companies surpassed $2.9 billion, outpacing adjacent sectors like fintech and defense tech. M&A remains the preferred exit path. Roughly 11% of Israeli cybersecurity startups are acquired early in their lifecycle, often within three to four years of founding, while IPOs remain rare. Big deals, including Google’s $32 billion acquisition of Wiz, have only cemented Israel’s dominance.

This success is impressive, but it has shaped investors’, founders’, and institutions’ expectations in specific ways.

Applied AI vs. Foundational AI

Israel’s AI ecosystem is growing rapidly. There are over 2,150 active AI companies, nearly half of all new startups founded in 2023, and AI ventures captured roughly 60% of total capital raised by Israeli startups that year. Yet most growth is in applied AI, solving vertical problems often using models developed elsewhere.

Applied AI is valuable, but it is not foundational. Foundational AI, which includes creating large language models, novel architectures, and model training infrastructure, is where global power is defined. Israel’s presence in this arena is minimal

Why Foundational AI Is Scarce

1. Path Dependence on Cyber
Israel’s cyber success created a powerful identity. Investors and founders chase what they know: security problems, enterprise demand, and M&A scalability.

2. Capital Intensity and Long Horizons
Developing foundational AI, especially competing with OpenAI, Google, or Chinese giants, requires massive capital, decades of research, and heavy compute infrastructure.

3. The “Exit Nation” Effect
In cyber, early acquisitions are the norm. Startups are often acquired in three to four years after founding. Why chase deep research and uncertain multi-year horizons when a strategic acquirer is nearby?

The result is an ecosystem thriving in applications and implementations, but without enough players building the core layers of tomorrow’s AI stack.

Global Context

The United States and China dominate foundational model research and infrastructure. Europe is attempting to build an AI ecosystem through regulation and investment. Israel, despite having the highest concentration of AI talent in the world according to the Stanford AI Index, still lacks a coherent position in the foundational layer.

Applied AI startups are flourishing, but most build around externally produced models rather than owning them.

Reframing the Narrative

If Israel wants to become an “AI Nation” rather than a cyber leader that uses AI, a shift is required:

  • Investment structures that support longer horizon research, including patient capital from institutional investors willing to take research risk.
  • Public-private alliances to scale compute infrastructure domestically rather than outsourcing to international cloud providers.
  • Academic-industry pipelines to retain AI researchers and incentivize foundational work within Israel.

Cyber and AI increasingly intersect, especially where AI introduces novel security threats. Israel’s cyber industry is evolving into AI-driven cybersecurity, creating high-value opportunities. But relying on the identity of “cyber nation” alone will not be enough. Foundational AI innovation is where future competitive advantage will be cemented.

Conclusion

Israel’s journey from “Start-Up Nation” to “Cyber Nation” was remarkable. The next transformation that is becoming an “AI Nation” requires rethinking success: fewer fast exits, more strategic bets on foundational technology, deeper research investments, and ecosystem incentives that value long-term dominance over short-term gains.

In the AI era, identity matters. Let Israel’s next chapter be defined not just by how well we defend the future, but by how boldly we build it.
